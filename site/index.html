Analysis of wind effect on audio recorders over three years of deployments.

01

Can the recordings hear the wind?

The station sits kilometres from most sites, so it reports the day’s weather rather than the air at the microphone. Even so, low-frequency level tracks it closely.

Mean level in the 20–200 Hz band, gain-corrected, against the station’s wind, pooled over all sites. The lower track is the share of recordings that clipped in each band — clipped audio cannot get any louder, which is what flattens the right-hand end of every curve above it.

2022–23 is drawn in grey on purpose. The station’s wind column changes scale in the winter before 2023–24: not one of the 32,944 readings before it exceeds 34 kt, while 2,146 of 51,968 after it do. Temperature, humidity and pressure are unchanged, and the ratio matches a 10-minute mean against a 10-minute maximum. 2023–24 and 2024–25 therefore compare directly; 2022–23 cannot share a wind axis with either.

04

What this does and does not establish

Four things could each have produced a confident wrong answer here: changing recorder gain, a clock in the wrong timezone, unequal weather between seasons, and an instrument change at the station. The first three are corrected. The fourth is bounded.

Supported

  • Low-frequency level tracks the anemometer at r = 0.55, against a station kilometres from most sites. Clock alignment was confirmed by a lag sweep, not assumed.
  • Which sites are exposed, and from which bearing — directly actionable for next season’s placement.
  • Season-over-season change at matched wind for 2023–24 against 2024–25, on shared sites and a shared wind scale.
  • Recording reliability, measured on every file written.

Not supported

  • A third to a half of recordings clip, rising to 93% above 40 kt. Differences between strong and very strong wind are understated everywhere.
  • The weather comes from one station. Shelter and distance from that station are not fully separable in these site differences.
  • No airborne site spans all three seasons, so the trend is a chain of overlapping pairs rather than one series.
  • Gain correction uses AudioMoth’s nominal 6 dB steps, not a calibration. Read 1–2 dB differences as noise.
  • Levels are dBFS, not sound pressure. They compare recordings to each other, not to an absolute acoustic scale.

One thing worth checking off the data. Whether the station’s anemometer or its logging changed before the 2023–24 season. A logbook would settle the scale break outright and unlock 2022–23 for direct comparison.
